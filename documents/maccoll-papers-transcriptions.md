## Janet Roberton MacColl — "Recollections of my beloved father"
**James Roberton, iron founder, of the Gorbals, who died 19 November 1868, aged 74.**
(Header added by a later hand: "Grandfather Roberton.")

> Recollections of the last days of my beloved father who died 19th Novr 1868 aged 74 years.
>
> In the beginning of October father had an illness which confined him for some days to bed and left him weak and frail. From this however he seemed to rally and looked remarkably well considering his advanced age and his feeble frame. He seemed to have no particular presentiment that his end was near, though he frequently remarked that he had passed the allotted period of man's life and could not be long with us.
>
> He had not been at church for several Sabbaths on account of his illness, but afterwards went one Sabbath to Mr Birkmyre's. On the following Sabbath, 15th Novr, he went to John St in a cab and thus paid a farewell visit to the place of worship where he had so often listened to the glad tidings of the gospel, and been refreshed by its ordinances, and where for nearly 40 years he had been an office bearer. On the afternoon [Dr] McEwen preached a sermon to children. On returning father went into the vestry to speak to Mrs Anderson. He met Mr McEwen and remarked to him, "That was an excellent sermon, and though I'm a pretty old child I enjoyed it very much."
>
> On that Sabbath evening John read to him the little memoir and death-bed sayings published by Dr Anderson about his little son Willie who died in July 1868. Father was in at business every day of that week, and on Thursday the [9th?] it was [remarked] by those who [saw] that he walked with a livelier step than usual. Besides being at the work he made several business calls and paid a short farewell visit to Mary (Mrs Christie). It was 5 o'clock when he came home. He told Jane the housekeeper that he had been at Mrs Christie's. She said he should not have gone that day, it was too much for him. He remarked, "I just thought I would go today, perhaps tomorrow will have enough to do with itself."
>
> He took a little dinner, then dozed a little in his easy chair. On rising to go to take to tea he remarked to Mary his granddaughter, "Well, I'm not rested yet, but I'll get a long rest after tea." After taking tea, in good spirits, Mary left him, little thinking she would never again see her dear grandpapa in life. Less than an hour afterwards he passed gently away without a struggle. Truly "he was not, because God took him."
>
> But what a shock to be called so unexpectedly to see him in his easy chair, motionless in the slumber of death. Those eyes which ever beamed on us with kindness now closed for ever. Those hands & feet which so often were engaged in doing good to his fellow creatures, no more to be engaged in errands of mercy. But blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ who hath begotten us again to a lively hope by the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead, to an inheritance incorruptible, undefiled, and unfading, away in the skies.
>
> We are left to mourn the loss of a kind & affectionate parent, and many [of us] his kind & sympathizing friendship. He was ever the friend of the poor and the helper of the needy. We cannot but feel grateful that he was enabled through sustaining grace to maintain his Christian profession & character to the end. And we may say of him: "I have fought the good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith. Henceforth there is laid for me a crown of righteousness, which the Lord the righteous Judge shall give me at that day, and not to me only, but unto all them also that love his appearing." May we be followers of them who through faith and patience now inherit the promises.
>
> J. McC[oll]

## Hugh MacColl to Janet Roberton — courtship letter (c. 1849)
**80 N. Frederick Street, Glasgow. "Friday night."**

> My Dearest Janet,
>
> After an hour & a half's preparation of my lesson for Sabbath, I have for the last 10 or 15 minutes been indulging in a kind of revery, retrospective and prospective. The truth is I have been thinking about yourself, by no means an uncommon thing as you may suppose, and it has occurred to me that as I could not see your face so often as I would like, there was nothing to prevent me thus to commune with you.
>
> And first let me thank you for your letter of Monday. It is kind, affectionate, faithful, just what I like, & would have expected from you. I appreciate highly your sympathy, & desire more than ever to recognise the hand of God in the late [trial]...

*(The remaining pages of Hugh's letters — L/D73/1/1/1/2/2, which concern "the affair of Miss Sartel" and the Ovendon family — are written in a small, faint, partly cross-written hand and are only partially legible from the scans; the originals are archived. A professional palaeographer could recover the full text.)*

## Mrs Janet Roberton (Dunoon) to her daughter Janet — 19 February 1851
Written by James Roberton's wife, "the Janet from Dunoon," to her married daughter ("Mrs McColl").

> Dunoon, 19th Feby 1851.
>
> Dear Janet,
>
> I was glad to hear from Father and Mary that you were keeping better; I hope you are improving in strength. How is little Jessie? I hope she is still thriving, dear little one, I think of her with much pleasure. I hope she will be one of those little ones who are sanctified from the womb; but as the seeds of corruption are inherent in our offspring, we ought to be earnest at a throne of grace for them from their infancy.
>
> I have been reading the life and memoir of Mrs Mary Lundie Duncan; she was a very remarkable person. Grace began to show itself when she was very young. She was endowed with great talents, and had great advantages, but she had the depravity of the human heart to contend with, and her only comfort at her end was the cross, which has afforded comfort to thousands and tens of thousands; and I trust you and I will be enabled at all times to say it is all [our] salvation and all our desire.
>
> I hope Mr McColl is well. I have enjoyed the retirement at this time very much. I have not wearied at all; indeed I think the time is flying fast, particularly when I think how little I have been doing of late. Father came down upon Saturday. The weather, which was delightful for the season, continued so till Monday forenoon, when it became changeable, and has been very stormy till this afternoon, when it has subsided all at once into a calm.
>
> On looking out upon the sea this afternoon, and contrasting it with its appearance in the forenoon when the waves were dashing upon the shore, I was led to adore the Sovereign power of God who alone can say to the sea, be calm. Man tries to imitate God in many things, but owns that he has no control. Hoping to see you soon, I remain, Dear Janet,
>
> Your affectionate Mother,
> J. Roberton
>
> Mrs McColl
