The MacColl Family

Mull to Glasgow to Rhode Island. Where the MacColls came from, traced from old family papers.

Somewhere back down the line a MacColl started saving paper and never stopped. Fifty feet of it, letters, certificates, photographs, and hand-drawn family trees running from 1811 to 1974, sits in the Lochaber Archive Centre in Fort William, Scotland, catalogued as collection GB3218 L/D73. I had a batch of it scanned, and it traces the family, in its own handwriting, back to the Isle of Mull in the 1700s. Here is the line, oldest first, with a family tree at the end.

The Isle of Mull, in the 1700s

The oldest names come off a tree that James Roberton MacColl, the mill man further down, drew up himself from a Dr. Hector MacColl of Tobermory. At its head are John MacColl, a farmer on the Isle of Mull, and his wife Margaret Campbell of Ardnamurchan, whose six children were born in the 1770s and scattered from Mull to Glasgow to Canada and Australia.

One of the Mull children, Hugh MacColl (born 1777), was a Glasgow clothier and tailor who married Agnes Currie. Two death records in the archive settle it: "Hugh McColl, tailor, 3 Germiston Street," who died in 1852 aged 74, and his wife "Ann [Agnes] Currie," who died in 1844 aged 64. The Curries go back one more step, to an Alexander Currie who died in 1806 aged 64.

Glasgow: tailors and an iron foundry

Hugh of 1777's son was another Hugh MacColl, a Glasgow tailor (1813-1882). In 1849 he married Janet Roberton in the Gorbals; the record names him "Hugh McColl Junr."

Janet's father, James Roberton, ran the Gorbals Iron Foundry, 52 men; an 1894 Glasgow history calls him "the much-esteemed Bailie Roberton," a city magistrate. So the tailor married up. Janet later wrote a plain, unsentimental account of the day her father died, in 1868, which is further down.

The tailor's son who built a mill

James Roberton MacColl, born in Glasgow in 1856, trained in the Glasgow textile trade and then, in February 1882, sailed for America on the S.S. Ethiopia out of Glasgow. He took over the Lorraine Mills in Pawtucket, Rhode Island and ran them for about fifty years, agent to treasurer to president; he was naturalized a US citizen at Providence in 1893, and in 1884 married a Glasgow girl, Agnes Bogle, raising six children in Rhode Island.

He did not stay small. Under him the Lorraine Mills ran about 2,000 workers and 2,750 looms, and took the only Grand Prix for fine colored goods at the Paris Exposition of 1900, against the best of Europe. He became national president of the American cotton manufacturers, chaired the 1919 World Cotton Conference, ran a Rhode Island bank, sat on the board of the US Chamber of Commerce, and headed Rhode Island's British war-relief effort in the First World War. When he died in Providence in 1931 the trade papers ran his obituary with a portrait. A Glasgow tailor's son who died a Yankee industrialist.

The families at the campout descend from one of James and Agnes's six children, Norman Alexander MacColl (born 1895), who in 1924 married Mary Kimbark of a prominent Chicago family, the one a Hyde Park avenue is named for. Norman and Mary's children started the four branches: Beckwith, Flynn, MacColl, and Bellows.

Norman's siblings kept up the mill-town pattern. One brother, Hugh, went to Harvard and became a composer. Two others, William and Kenneth, married the two daughters of Alfred M. Coats, who ran the enormous J. & P. Coats thread works in Pawtucket, so the MacColls married straight into the family that owned the thread empire across town.

The wars left a lighter mark. All five of James's grown sons registered for the First World War draft, though no combat service is recorded for any of them, and a grandson, James Roberton MacColl III, served as a Navy chaplain in the Second.

The voices

The papers hold more than names. Hugh's courtship letters to Janet before their 1849 wedding, a letter to Janet from her mother in Dunoon, and Janet's account of the night her father, the iron founder James Roberton, died in 1868.

"On rising to go to take to tea he remarked to Mary his granddaughter, 'Well, I'm not rested yet, but I'll get a long rest after tea.' After taking tea, in good spirits, Mary left him, little thinking she would never again see her dear grandpapa in life. Less than an hour afterwards he passed gently away without a struggle."

And Hugh, courting Janet: "I have been thinking about yourself, by no means an uncommon thing as you may suppose, and it has occurred to me that as I could not see your face so often as I would like, there was nothing to prevent me thus to commune with you."

The archive

The MacColl papers are catalogued, and anyone in the family can order copies. Email the Lochaber Archive Centre at lochaber.archives@highlifehighland.com and ask about collection GB3218 / L/D73, the MacColl papers. It runs about £2.75 per scanned page, emailed to you. A good deal of the collection is still unscanned, including Janet Roberton's personal diaries and years of James's letters home from Rhode Island.
