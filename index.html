The MacColl Family

Mull to Glasgow to Rhode Island. Where our family actually comes from, put together from the old family papers, for all of us who gather at the campout.

Every family has stories about where it came from. Ours we can actually prove, because at some point a MacColl started saving paper and never stopped. Fifty feet of it, letters, certificates, photographs, and hand-drawn family trees, sits today in the Lochaber Archive Centre in Fort William, Scotland, catalogued as collection GB3218 L/D73. This past year we got a batch of it scanned, and it lets us trace the family, in its own handwriting, all the way back to the Isle of Mull in the 1700s. Here it is, oldest first, and there's a family tree further down for finding your own branch.

The Isle of Mull, in the 1700s

The oldest names come off a tree that James Roberton MacColl, the mill man you'll meet in a minute, drew up himself, from a Dr. Hector MacColl of Tobermory on Mull. At its head stand John MacColl, a farmer on the Isle of Mull, and his wife Margaret Campbell of Ardnamurchan, whose six children were born in the 1770s and scattered from Mull to Glasgow to Canada and Australia.

One of those Mull children was Hugh MacColl, born 1777, a Glasgow clothier and tailor, who married Agnes Currie. The family wondered about this rung for a hundred years; the old death records in the archive finally settle it, "Hugh McColl, tailor, 3 Germiston Street," who died in 1852 aged 74, and his wife "Ann [Agnes] Currie," who died in 1844 aged 64.

Glasgow: tailors and an iron foundry

Hugh of 1777's son was another Hugh MacColl, a tailor in Glasgow (1813-1882). In 1849 he married Janet Roberton in the Gorbals, and their marriage record is one of the scanned papers.

Janet brought foundry blood into the family. Her father, James Roberton, ran the Gorbals Iron Foundry, employing 52 men; an 1894 Glasgow history calls him "the much-esteemed Bailie Roberton." He was a magistrate and a man of standing, and his daughter left a moving handwritten account of the day he died, in 1868, which you can read in the voices further down.

The couple who brought us to America

One of Hugh and Janet's sons changed everything. James Roberton MacColl, born in Glasgow on April 2, 1856, trained in textiles, crossed to America in 1882, and took over the Lorraine Mills in Pawtucket, Rhode Island, which he ran for essentially the next fifty years, agent to treasurer to president. He married Agnes Bogle in 1884, and together they raised six children in Rhode Island. He grew into a genuinely big figure in American textiles, and when he died at home in Providence in 1931, the industry papers ran his obituary with his portrait. A Glasgow tailor's son who died a Yankee industrialist.

Our own line runs through one of those six children, Norman Alexander MacColl (born 1895), who in 1924 married Mary Kimbark, of a well-known Chicago family. Norman and Mary are the great-grandparents every one of us at the campout shares, and their children became the families we belong to today, the Beckwiths, the Flynns, the MacColls, and the Bellowses. (Norman's siblings were a lively bunch too: four brothers and a little sister who died young, one brother a Harvard composer, and two of them married into the Coats mill family.)

And the archive gave up their faces, the oldest photographs any of us have of this family.

The voices

The archive holds more than names. It holds Hugh's courtship letters to Janet before their 1849 wedding, a letter to Janet from her own mother, and Janet's tender handwritten "Recollections of my beloved father," the iron founder James Roberton. For the first time, we can hear them.

"On rising to go to take to tea he remarked to Mary his granddaughter, 'Well, I'm not rested yet, but I'll get a long rest after tea.' After taking tea, in good spirits, Mary left him, little thinking she would never again see her dear grandpapa in life. Less than an hour afterwards he passed gently away without a struggle. Truly, he was not, because God took him."

And Hugh, courting Janet before their wedding: "I have been thinking about yourself, by no means an uncommon thing as you may suppose, and it has occurred to me that as I could not see your face so often as I would like, there was nothing to prevent me thus to commune with you."

Help fill in the gaps

This is a start, not a finish. If you have old MacColl photographs, letters, or stories at home, or if you can name any of the faces above, please share them. The more of us who add what we have, the more complete the family's story gets. Bring what you've got to the campout.
