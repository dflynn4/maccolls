The MacColl Family

Mull to Glasgow to Rhode Island. Where the MacColls came from, traced from old family papers.

Somewhere back down the line a MacColl started saving paper and never stopped. Fifty feet of it, letters, certificates, photographs, and hand-drawn family trees running from 1811 to 1974, sits in the Lochaber Archive Centre in Fort William, Scotland, catalogued as collection GB3218 L/D73. I had a batch of it scanned, and it traces the family, in its own handwriting, back to the Isle of Mull in the 1700s. Here is the line, oldest first, with a family tree at the end.

The Isle of Mull, in the 1700s

The oldest names come off a tree that James Roberton MacColl, the mill man further down, drew up himself from a Dr. Hector MacColl of Tobermory. At its head are John MacColl, a farmer on the Isle of Mull, and his wife Mary Campbell, whose children were born in the 1770s. That tree is no longer the only evidence. The parish register of Kilninian and Kilmore records four children born to the couple — Mary in March 1769, Duncan in November 1774, Hugh in July 1777 and Janet in July 1781 — so the oldest names in this family are now in the official index to the Scottish registers, with both parents named, and not just in a descendant’s handwriting. (The tree says six children; the register of that parish, which begins in 1766, shows four. Four is what can be proved.)

The MacColls were a small Highland clan, an offshoot of the MacDonalds, from Appin on the mainland just across the water from Mull. They were the fighting men of the Stewarts of Appin, and they paid in blood for it: of the 157 casualties the Appin regiment took for Bonnie Prince Charlie at Culloden, thirty-three were MacColls — eighteen killed and fifteen wounded, and among the rank and file that was nearly a third of the losses, more than twice any other name. By the 1770s John MacColl was farming on Mull, in the parish of Kilninian and Kilmore near Tobermory, a Gaelic-speaking place that lived off cattle, driving the herds south to sell each fall. But that whole way of life was already breaking apart, the landlords carving up the old shared farms and driving the rents up, right as John's children were being born.

What happened to those six children is the whole Highland story in a nutshell. Within two generations the MacColls were gone from Mull, scattered to Canada (some to a Gaelic-speaking town in Ontario the tree calls "Finch, near Montreal"), some to Australia, and one, Hugh's line, to Glasgow. This was the Clearances: across the 1800s Mull lost about four-fifths of its people as the landlords cleared the farms for sheep. Tobermory became a port people sailed away from, and charities shipped thousands of Highlanders off to Australia, where their knack with cattle and sheep was worth something. The MacColls left with everyone else.

One of the six, Hugh MacColl, was christened in that Mull parish on 3 July 1777, son of John MacColl and Mary Campbell. He took the shorter road, to Glasgow, where tailoring was one of the first trades a Highland newcomer could get into. He settled north of the river: his address at his death was 3 Germiston Street, out beyond St Rollox on the north side of the Monkland Canal, and he was buried from St David's in the Merchant City. It was his son who moved south of the Clyde to the Gorbals. He became a tailor and married Agnes Currie. Two death records in the archive close the loop: "Hugh MacColl, tailor, 3 Germiston Street," who died in 1852 aged 74, and his wife "Ann [Agnes] Currie," who died in 1844 aged 64, and the Curries go back one step further, to an Alexander Currie who died in 1806. That little tailor's shop in the Gorbals was the start of everything, the mill, the Rhode Island fortune, all of it. His baptism sits at 544/10/45 in the Kilninian register, and there is no rival: he is the only Hugh MacColl baptised to a John in that parish in those years.

Glasgow: tailors and an iron foundry

Hugh married Agnes Currie in Glasgow on 1 November 1802, and the register shows a household far larger than the family ever knew about. Nine children were baptised to the couple in the city parish between 1805 and 1822: Alexander (1805), John (1807), Mary (1808), Daniel (1810), Agnes (1811), Hugh (8 May 1813), Charles (1815), a second Agnes (1820) and Duncan (1822). The second Agnes is the sad tell — a name is only re-used when the first child carrying it has died. The Hugh this line runs through was the sixth of nine, not an only son.

That younger Hugh MacColl, a Glasgow tailor (1813-1882), is now nailed down from both ends. His baptism names his parents as Hugh MacColl and Agnes Currie; his death record, registered in Tradeston in 1882, gives his age as sixty-nine and his mother's maiden name as Currie. Born 1813, died 1882 at sixty-nine: the arithmetic closes. His sister Mary does the same trick independently — baptised October 1808, she died in Tradeston in 1855 aged forty-seven, mother's maiden name Currie, in the very district the family lived in.

On 13 September 1849 he married Janet Roberton. The wedding was entered twice, once in each of their parishes, as was normal then: in the Gorbals register, where the record names him "Hugh MacColl Junr.", and again across the river in Glasgow.

Janet's father, James Roberton, ran the Gorbals Iron Foundry, 52 men; an 1894 Glasgow history calls him "the much-esteemed Bailie Roberton," a city magistrate. So the tailor married up. Janet later wrote a plain, unsentimental account of the day her father died, in 1868, which is further down.

"Tailor" undersells him. The Scottish censuses show what the household was actually like. In 1861 the family was at 38 South Apsley Place, Tradeston — Hugh 47, Janet 34, four small children, and two live-in servants. By 1871 they had moved up to a named house, Auburn Cottage on St Andrews Road, with three more children and a servant. Hugh was a master of his trade, not a journeyman, which is rather the point: the foundry-owner's daughter did not marry a poor man.

The 1881 census finds them at 10 Kenmure Street, and two things have changed. Janet is gone, and Hugh is a widower at sixty-seven. And the household still holds five of their grown children, among them a twenty-five-year-old son who gives his occupation as dress manufacturer. Ten months after that census was taken, he sailed for America.

Janet had died on 27 December 1871 — the year James turned fifteen and left school for the warehouse. The boy who went behind a draper's counter that year had just lost his mother. Hugh outlived her by eleven years: born 8 May 1813, he died in Glasgow on 12 December 1882, ten months after his son sailed for Rhode Island. They never saw one another again. And there is one thing known about the kind of man he was, recorded by another of his sons: he was a Presbyterian elder and a Sunday school teacher for over fifty years.

A set of certificates in the archive carries the Robertons two generations further back, and out of Glasgow altogether. James Roberton was born on 4 May 1794 and baptised that June at Balfron, in Stirlingshire. The register names his father: John Roberton, printer. John had married Catharine Roberton — the same surname as his own — at Bonhill in Dumbartonshire on 25 April 1789, and the banns describe him as "John Roberton at Levenbank." Catharine was herself baptised in April 1760 at Carmunnock, Lanarkshire, daughter of James Roberton and Margaret McFarlane. The iron founder's own death certificate closes the circle: he died at Pollokshields on 19 November 1868, aged 74, which matches the 1794 birth exactly, and the death was reported by his son John, Janet's brother.

"Printer at Levenbank" is worth pausing on. Levenbank sits in the Vale of Leven, which in the 1790s was the heart of Scotland's calico-printing and Turkey-red dyeing trade. He printed cloth, not books. So this family was already in textiles a generation before the iron foundry, and two generations before a MacColl took charge of a cotton mill in Rhode Island.

The tailor's son who built a mill

James Roberton MacColl was born in Tradeston, Glasgow, in 1856 — the registrar wrote him down as "James Robertson McColl," getting his mother's surname slightly wrong, and he spent the rest of his life spelling it her way. He was educated at Anderson Academy, Glasgow High School and the Glasgow Technical College (later the Royal Technical College, now the University of Strathclyde), and went into cloth with Henry Fyfe & Son, makers of cotton and woollen dress goods. He had begun even earlier than that: the 1871 census, taken when he was fifteen, already gives his occupation as clerk, drapery. His whole career started behind a counter measuring cloth. By 1878, at twenty-two, he was in business for himself as a partner with John Thomson in Thomson & MacColl. On a visit to America in 1881 he met the brothers W. F. and F. C. Sayles, who were just then starting the Lorraine Manufacturing Company, and in February 1882 he sailed for good on the S.S. Ethiopia out of Glasgow to be their agent. Why a firm in Rhode Island handed a mill to a twenty-five-year-old Scot is no longer a mystery: it was deliberate policy. A 1908 account of Frederic C. Sayles says that at Lorraine and the Glenlyon Dye Works "the very best skilled foremen for each department were engaged from abroad by him before any movement was made to build the works." The Sayles method was to buy the European expertise first and build the factory around it. James was part of the plant. He ran the Lorraine Mills in Pawtucket, Rhode Island for the next forty-nine years: agent; then, from 1896, when the Sayles family stepped back, a large shareholder, treasurer and secretary; then president from 1920. He was naturalized as an American citizen at Providence on 17 December 1895, aged thirty-nine. In April 1884 he married Agnes Bogle in Glasgow. She was born in Tradeston on 15 September 1859, three years after James and in the same district, the daughter of William Bogle and Margaret Yuill — her birth is registered at Tradeston under her mother's maiden name, and so are three of her brothers, John in Tradeston in 1856 and Andrew and David Robert in Govan in 1862 and 1865, which traces the family's move across the river year by year. Her parents had married in July 1845, and their wedding too was entered twice: in the Gorbals, where they lived, and at East Kilbride in Lanarkshire, which is the first hint anyone has ever had of where the Bogles or the Yuills came from. Agnes came out to Rhode Island that year and stayed for the rest of her life. They raised six children in Pawtucket and Providence: Hugh (1885), William (1887), James (1891), Norman (1895) and Kenneth (1898), and a daughter, Margaret, born in 1888, who died the day after Christmas 1893, aged five. Five sons outlived him. So did Agnes, by six years: she died in Providence on 29 November 1937, and they lie together at Swan Point.

Almost nothing survives about Agnes herself — no letter, no obituary anyone has been able to find — but in April 1922 she and James applied for passports on the same day to sail for Britain, and the form required a description. She was sixty-two, five feet five and a quarter inches tall, with a high forehead, a straight nose, an oval face and hair still recorded as brown. James, on the facing page, was five feet five and a half, with grey hair and blue eyes: they were within a quarter of an inch of one another. Pinned to the foot of her application is a photograph. They sailed from Boston on the Laconia on 31 May 1922, and she signed the form in a firm hand, Agnes Bogle MacColl.

She had made the same application two years earlier, in April 1920, and that one is clearer. It gives her at sixty: five feet five, medium forehead, grey eyes, brown hair, an oval face, no distinguishing marks. James, alongside, had blue eyes and hair described as light brown turning grey. They were the same height to half an inch. The photograph attached to the 1920 form is the best likeness of Agnes that survives anywhere — a woman of sixty, dark hair drawn back off a broad forehead, in a dark dress with a pale patterned collar. Her identity was sworn to by Arthur L. Brown, United States District Judge for Rhode Island, who said he had known her personally for fifteen years. A federal judge vouching for a Glasgow girl who had come over in 1884 is its own quiet measure of how far the family had travelled.

He did not stay small. Under him the Lorraine Mills ran about 2,000 workers and 2,750 looms, and won at the Paris Exposition of 1900. That had always been a family story; it is now a matter of public record. The United States government's own report on the exposition lists, in its schedule of awards, "Lorraine Manufacturing Company, Pawtucket, R. I. Grand prize in class 80" — threads and fabrics of cotton — and, on the same page, a silver medal in class 82, for worsteds, which nobody in the family seems to have known about at all. The catalogue describes what they sent: fine cotton fabrics in woven colors. Three American grand prizes were given in that class; the other two went to plain white goods, so Lorraine's was the only American grand prize for fine coloured cotton. The juror on wool went out of his way to name the mill, writing that they "deserve special praise" for "goods of more varied character, and of a higher order than is generally made in the United States." The mill paid $1,600 to put the exhibit up. He was president of the New England Cotton Manufacturers' Association from 1905 to 1907, and it was on his watch that it widened its reach and became the National Association of Cotton Manufacturers; his son William held the same presidency in 1925–27. He chaired the executive committee of the 1919 World Cotton Conference in New Orleans, ran a Rhode Island bank, sat on the board of the US Chamber of Commerce, and in the First World War was president of the British Relief Association of Rhode Island and sat on the state's Fuel Administration. When he died in Providence in 1931 the trade papers ran his obituary with a portrait. A Glasgow tailor's son who died a Yankee industrialist.

The largest surviving block of his own words is the printed report of the 1919 World Cotton Conference at New Orleans, which he opened. He was not a diplomatic speaker. On the commodity exchanges: "Any system of cotton exchange trading which… places the cotton industry at the mercy of gamblers should be eliminated." On the state of the trade's own packaging: "A decent American bale is certainly one of the crying needs of the industry. No one has the hardihood to defend the present bale." And in the middle of a floor debate, losing patience: "Any woolen man knows that is perfectly absurd." Thirteen years earlier, in his president's address to the cotton manufacturers, he had turned the same bluntness on his own countrymen: "In many of our factories quality is sacrificed to quantity and cheapness, and designing consists in clever copying of foreign samples." Read next to the Paris grand prize, that is a man saying exactly what he thought he had been hired to fix.

Two other things survive of him in his own voice, and they sit oddly together. At the cotton manufacturers' convention in September 1906, in the middle of a technical argument about steam turbines, a speaker gave up on a comic poem in broad Scots — "a dialect which I do not feel familiar enough with to do justice" — and handed it to MacColl, who said he would "be very glad to make the effort" and read all five stanzas of "The Receeprocatin' Mon" aloud. Twenty-four years in Rhode Island, and they still turned to him for the accent.

And from the chair at the same meeting, introducing a lecture on child labour, he said: "We cannot operate our factories without child labor and every restrictive law tends to make it more difficult." It is printed in the volume's own index under his name. He believed the minimum age should rise, but by the industry's own choice rather than by law. It is the plainest statement of his politics anywhere in the record, and it belongs here next to the poem.

His death made the national wire. The same obituary ran on 23 and 24 November 1931 in Boston, Philadelphia, Atlanta, Chattanooga, Springfield, New Bedford, Vermont and North Carolina, and it lists a good deal more than the mill. He was vice president and a director of the Industrial Trust Company, then the largest bank in Rhode Island; president of the Ponemah Mills in Connecticut, of the Morris Plan Company of Rhode Island, and of the Moshassuck Valley Railroad Company. He was elected president of the New England Cotton Manufacturers Association in 1905, presided over the International Conference of Cotton Growers and Manufacturers at Washington the year after, and chaired the same conference at Atlanta in October 1907 — which is why an Atlanta paper still ran his death twenty-four years later. When the Pawtucket textile millionaire Frank A. Sayles died in 1920, MacColl was made executor of his will and a trustee of his estate.

He died early on 23 November 1931 at his home, 260 Waterman Street, Providence, aged 75, and was buried from the Central Congregational Church two days later. The Philadelphia papers carried it because his brother, the Rev. Alexander MacColl, was pastor of the Second Presbyterian Church in that city. The obituary notes he was survived by his wife, five sons, two sisters and two brothers.

The Glasgow Herald of 27 June 1931 prints the annual prize list of Glasgow High School, and among the awards are a "James R. MacColl essay prize" and a "James R. MacColl essay competition." The list does not say who endowed them, so it is not quite proof. But his trade-paper obituary confirms he was a Glasgow High School boy himself, and an old boy who had made a fortune, with prizes in his name at his own school being handed out five months before he died, is about as close as it gets.

The families at the campout all descend from one of James and Agnes's five sons, Norman Alexander MacColl (1895–1988). Norman grew up in the Rhode Island mill town and, in June 1924, in Evanston, Illinois, married Mary Kimbark (1903–1969), of a well-known Chicago family, the one a Hyde Park avenue is still named for. They raised five children in Rhode Island, and it is those five who started the branches at the campout: Louise, born July 1925, who married a Bellows; Nancy, November 1926, who married a Beckwith; Joan, March 1930, who married a Flynn; and the brothers Norman Alexander junior, April 1934, and Hugh Frederick the second, May 1937, named for the composer, who kept the MacColl name. Mary died in 1969. Norman lived another nineteen years, and did not spend them alone: within about a year of losing her he married again, at seventy-four, to Dorothy Arnold Harris — "Dot" — and moved down the bay to Saunderstown. She was a Rhode Islander too, born in Providence on 20 May 1898 to Edward E. Arnold and Mittie Hodges, and widowed or divorced from a Mr. Harris before she met him. The date of the wedding is fixed by an unlikely source: the Social Security Administration logged her name as Harris in January 1963 and as MacColl by July 1970, and Mary had died in March 1969. He died there on 15 October 1988, aged ninety-three. His death notice in the Boston Globe names her as his wife and lists his five children, and the memorial service was held in the chapel at Swan Point, where his parents, his sister Margaret who died at five, and Mary are all buried. He asked that gifts go to the Rhode Island Foundation. Dorothy outlived him by less than five months, dying on 3 March 1989 at ninety. They are not buried together: she lies across the city in the North Burial Ground, and he lies at Swan Point with his first wife and his parents.

Norman's own life is on the record in more detail than anyone's in this story. He went into the family firm. By 1930, at thirty-four, he chaired the nominating committee of the cotton manufacturers' association his father had once led; by 1943 he was president of Lorraine, and in 1950 the census-taker wrote him down as "president, textile mill." He also presided over its end. In October 1944 he sold the cotton-and-rayon plant to Jacob Ziskind of Fall River and kept the worsted mill going; in July 1953, the week after his daughter Joan's wedding, he told the press that Lorraine was taking no more orders and running out its stock, as the New England worsted trade went south. The company's records end in 1954. James took the mill over in 1882, his sons ran it, and Norman closed it, seventy-one years on. Afterwards he sat on the board of the Industrial National Bank, his occupation given as trustee, into his seventies.

He also stayed close to the university he had walked out of. He belonged to the Yale Club in New York, sat on the executive committee of the Yale Association of Rhode Island, and chaired its scholarship committee — a man with no degree of his own raising money to send other Rhode Island boys to New Haven. His clubs at home were the Hope and the Agawam Hunt, the same two his father had been president of, along with the Jacobs Hill Hunt, the Warwick Country and Wianno.

Off the job he sailed. He raced a Wianno Senior called Halcyon, won the Wianno Senior series in her in August 1933, and in 1949 was elected commodore of the Wianno Yacht Club. Wianno, on the south shore of Cape Cod, is where this family summered for three generations: James belonged to the club, Agnes was there with Norman's family in 1936, the Bronxville and Pennsylvania cousins came too, and it is where Norman Jr. met Nancy Herron, whose Washington family kept a house on the same beach. After his father died at 260 Waterman Street in November 1931, Norman moved his own family into the house, and they were still there in 1938; by 1940 they had gone round the corner to Laurel Avenue, where the household ran to twelve at that year's census, five children and three live-in staff, one of whom, Bridie Shiel from Ireland, was still with them ten years later. And he and Mary collected American pressed glass, seriously: in 1961 they gave the Bennington Museum in Vermont 1,242 goblets, one of every pattern they could find, and the museum was still showing it as "the Norman A. MacColl collection" twenty years later.

Of the four, the Flynn branch runs through Joan, born in Providence on 10 March 1930. She married Donald White Flynn Jr. on 27 June 1953 — he had been born on 9 March 1930, one day before her. He died on 7 January 1973 at forty-two, and she was left a widow at the same age; she married again, to a Brennan, and was living in Needham, Massachusetts by the 1990s. Her son John Matthew died in 1993 at thirty-one. Joan died on 20 June 2013, aged eighty-three, and lies at Glenwood Cemetery in East Greenwich with her first husband and her son. The stone carries all three of her names: Joan MacColl Flynn Brennan.

One thing recorded plainly, because it is on the record: in November 1945 he was one of fifteen Rhode Island company heads who signed a petition to Congress against a slate of post-war bills — national health insurance, federal aid to schools, unemployment compensation, and the bill to make the wartime Fair Employment Practice Committee permanent — calling them "the trend toward national socialism." He was a Republican mill-owner of his time and place, and he put his name to it.

Norman's siblings kept up the mill-town pattern. One brother, Hugh Frederick MacColl (born Pawtucket, 1885), really did go to Harvard and really was a composer, and the family has understated him. He studied violin, piano and organ as a boy in Pawtucket, went to St. Paul's School in Concord, New Hampshire from 1897 to 1903, where he served unofficially as assistant organist, and then read music at Harvard, taking his degree magna cum laude in 1907. He was a charter member of the University Glee Club of Providence and its accompanist from 1911 to 1936, a trustee of the New England Conservatory of Music, and sat on the visiting committee of the music department at Brown. He wrote sixteen works between 1927 and 1945 — four for orchestra (Arabs, a Ballad for orchestra and piano, a Romantic Suite in the Form of Variations, and a Noël), five choral and seven chamber. The Providence Symphony under Wassili Leps premiered him in November 1932 and again in April 1935, the WPA Rhode Island Symphony in December 1940, and his music was broadcast on WNYC, WMCA, WEAF and WEAN. And all the while he was a businessman: in business from 1907, and senior partner of MacColl, Fraser & Wheeler, investment bankers of Providence. He is one of the composers profiled in Claire Reis's Composers in America (Macmillan, 1947).

He married Margery MacKillop in Pawtucket in May 1915 and they had three daughters — Margaret, Adele, and a third they named Janet Roberton MacColl, carrying the Glasgow foundry family's surname forward as a girl's given name in Rhode Island in 1921. Hugh died on 17 October 1953, aged sixty-eight, and lies at Swan Point in the same group as his parents.

Two things of his outlasted him. Brown University's John Hay Library holds the Hugh F. MacColl Papers — some nine linear feet of manuscripts, scores, phonograph records and scrapbooks — and his portrait in oils hangs in the university's music building. And in 1954, out of his bequest, Harvard founded the Hugh F. MacColl Prize for original musical composition by undergraduates. It is still awarded every year. A tailor's great-grandson from the Gorbals pays for music at Harvard, seventy years after his death.

A third son went into the church. Alexander MacColl, born in Glasgow on 27 December 1866 — the four-year-old in the 1871 census — became a Presbyterian minister in America and, for many years, pastor of the Second Presbyterian Church, Philadelphia. He was nominated for Moderator of the General Assembly, the denomination's highest office, in 1919. He was also a modernist, and in October 1934 his name headed a list of eleven Philadelphia ministers charged with heresy for signing the Auburn Affirmation; he kept his pulpit for another fifteen years and died in 1953, aged eighty-six. He married Grant Stuart Hally Craig in Glasgow in 1892, and it was he who came out to Evanston in 1924 to help marry his nephew Norman.

Philadelphia's evening paper followed him closely enough that you can hear him. In March 1915, when the revivalist Billy Sunday was filling a tabernacle in the city and most clergy were falling in behind him, MacColl publicly refused, objecting to the "breaches of Christian courtesy" in the campaign and to conduct "against the spirit of Christ." Preaching the baccalaureate at Lafayette College in 1916, with the war on in Europe and America still out of it, he told the graduating class there was "a lot of sprawling earnestness in the world today," and that "all the fundamental causes of the horror abroad are here in embryo in our American life." In 1920 he chaired the executive committee of the General Assembly and introduced the Governor and the Mayor from the stage of the Academy of Music, and served as honorary vice-chairman of a committee for the relief of German children — two years after the armistice. His daughter Ailsa went to Scotland in 1917 to nurse the wounded. He lived at 2108 Delancey Street, four doors from the house of Justice von Moschzisker, which anarchists bombed on the last day of 1918. He is buried at Laurel Hill West in Bala Cynwyd, section Oxford, lot 114, and the cemetery's own record puts three of them in that one lot: Alexander himself, 1866–1953; his wife Grant Stuart Hally Craig MacColl, 1865–1959, who outlived him by six years; and their daughter Ailsa — the one who sailed for Scotland in 1917 to nurse — who married a Pender and died on 14 January 1978. The fourth brother stayed in Scotland and can now be named: the Rev. John McColl, M.A., born in Glasgow on 30 September 1863, Glasgow High School like his brothers, an M.A. of Glasgow University at eighteen, and from 1886 to 1910 the first minister of Lylesland church in Paisley. He then went out to Queenstown, South Africa for ten years, came home to country charges in Aberdeenshire, and died in 1944. Two independent records fix him: the register of his birth, which gives his mother's maiden name as Roberton, and the printed roll of Scottish ministers, which names his parents outright as "Hugh Mccoll and Janet Roberton." So the Glasgow tailor put two sons into the ministry and one into a Rhode Island cotton mill. With the two sisters, Jessie (born August 1850, eleven months after the wedding) and Agnes (June 1852), that is the whole family — and it matches, exactly, the line in James's 1931 obituary saying he left "two sisters and two brothers."

William (who died in 1941) and Kenneth (1898–1971) married the two daughters of Alfred M. Coats, who ran the enormous J. & P. Coats thread works in Pawtucket, so the MacColls married straight into the family that owned the thread empire across town. By 1931 William was treasurer of Lorraine and Kenneth vice-president and treasurer of J. & P. Coats (R.I.). Another brother, James Jr., married Louise Kimbark in 1917 — the sister of the Mary Kimbark whom Norman would marry seven years later. Two brothers, two sisters: the same pattern twice. His Princeton classmates called him Bob, and their twenty-fifth-year book puts the wedding and the war in one sentence: "In 1917 — the same year he hied himself to war on the decks of the U. S. S. Ontario — Bob married Louise Kimbark." So while Norman was driving ambulances in France, his brother was at sea. He is usually described as a vice-president of Lorraine, but that needs a date on it: by 1939 he had left the family mill and was vice-president of Federated Textiles, Inc. in New York, and the class book files him under "our cotton-broking coterie."

The First World War reached them. On 18 May 1917, six weeks after America entered the war, Norman swore out a passport application in Providence. He was twenty-one, and where the form asks for an occupation he wrote "student at Yale." Under "country" he wrote France; under "object of visit," ambulance service; and he stated that he would leave New York on the French Line on 26 May 1917. He sailed eight days later, as a volunteer, before there was any army to send him. The application still carries his photograph: a boy in a dark suit and a stiff collar, looking straight at the camera. Three weeks after he sailed, the draft registration reached his family in Providence, and his brother William had to sign the card on his behalf, because Norman was already in France. His brothers all registered too. A generation later a grandson, James Roberton MacColl III, served as a Navy chaplain in the Second World War.

He was decorated. An army award card gives him as "MacColl, Norman A. — 9,700, Pvt. 1cl., Section No. 4/627 Amb. Service," and records the French Croix de Guerre with bronze star, under French Order No. 12.000 "D" dated 29 November 1918, "for act of July 18–24, 1918." He was twenty-two. The section's own muster roll for 31 December 1917 carries his line, MacColl, Norman A., Sept 24/17: he was sworn into the Army in France that day with most of the section, the month the volunteers became soldiers, on the same page as the two men the unit history mourns.

His Yale classmates wrote him up in their class book that year, and it is the closest thing we have to Norman described by people who knew him. He had come to Yale from St. Paul's School in Concord, New Hampshire and Worcester Academy. St Paul's own alumni directory dates that exactly: all five brothers went there, and Norman entered in 1908 and left in 1911, four years before the Form he had started with graduated, which is why Worcester Academy sits between school and Yale. Hugh and William had gone up together in 1897 and stayed six years; the youngest, Kenneth, lasted a single year. At Yale Norman was reading the Select Course in the Sheffield Scientific School with the class of 1918, and was reading the Select Course in the Sheffield Scientific School with the class of 1918. He belonged to Theta Xi and roomed with two classmates, Harold Cheel and a man named Holden. And he played first violin in the Yale University Orchestra in 1915–16 — the musical streak that ran through his brother Hugh ran through him too. Then, in the book's own words: "MacColl left the University in May, 1917, and went to France with the Yale Unit of the Ambulance Service, later reënlisting in U. S. Army Ambulance, and being assigned to S. S. U. 627."

He never finished the course — but Yale gave him the degree anyway, and the reason is worth setting down. The Yale Corporation had ruled that a Sheffield man who withdrew to join the Army and who could "present a meritorious service record" might take his degree honoris causa, submitting that record "in lieu of additional residence." Norman was a private and never held a commission, so it was the service record he offered — the one with the Croix de Guerre on it. The university's Catalogue of Officers and Graduates duly lists him among the Bachelors of Philosophy, honoris causa, Class of 1918. Yale took his war in place of his senior year. His youngest brother Kenneth followed him to Sheffield and graduated with the class of 1921.

Those dates place him precisely. The two numbers on his card are the same unit twice: the volunteer American Field Service Section Four, which in September 1917 enlisted whole into the United States Army as Section 627. The section published its own history after the war, and it describes that week. Attached to the 1st French Infantry Division, it went into the line in the Forest of Villers-Cotterêts, north-east of Paris. On 18 July 1918 the division attacked out of that forest in the first line of assault, behind tanks — General Mangin's surprise counter-blow, the attack that stopped the German advance and turned the war. The ambulances followed the advance through Longpont, Villers-Hélon and Saint-Rémy-Blanzy to a village called le Plessier-Huleu, which the French took on 23 July.

"The hottest spot was le Plessier-Huleu. There many of the men had to drive through almost a barrage to get to the poste."

Twenty years later his Yale class published a follow-up volume, and Norman set down his own war in four lines. He went to France in May 1917 with S.S.U. 64, the Yale Unit; enlisted as a private in the U.S. Army Ambulance Service that September; and was posted to S.S.U. 4 and S.S.U. 627, the same section under both its numbers. Then he listed where he had been: Verdun, Compiègne, Villers-Cotterêts, l'Assigny and Alsace. It is the only surviving account of his war in his own words, and it is that short.

That is the section's own account of the ground Norman's medal was given for. The award was a collective one — the history records that the section "received its citation for the work it did during the attack at Villers-Cotterêts," and each man got the cross. Afterwards the section went to Alsace, then marched to the Rhine and wintered at Gross-Gerau in Germany before being ordered home in February 1919. Army paperwork matches: Norman is on a holding detachment's roll on 14 February 1919, and sailed for home on the USS Pueblo on 16 March 1919, with his father, James R. MacColl of Providence, listed as his contact.

A caution for anyone using this: the section's published history is the unit's account, not Norman's. He is not named in it — he came to the section from the Yale unit, not from the Field Service whose men that book rosters — so nothing above is in his own words. It is his unit, his week and the citation he was decorated under — but it is not his own account, and no source here quotes him.

In their own hand

The box also held handwriting: Hugh's courtship letters to Janet before their 1849 wedding, a letter to Janet from her mother in Dunoon, and Janet's account of the night her father, the iron founder James Roberton, died in 1868.

"On rising to go to take to tea he remarked to Mary his granddaughter, 'Well, I'm not rested yet, but I'll get a long rest after tea.' After taking tea, in good spirits, Mary left him, little thinking she would never again see her dear grandpapa in life. Less than an hour afterwards he passed gently away without a struggle."

And Hugh, courting Janet: "I have been thinking about yourself, by no means an uncommon thing as you may suppose, and it has occurred to me that as I could not see your face so often as I would like, there was nothing to prevent me thus to commune with you."

The archive

The MacColl papers are catalogued, and anyone in the family can order copies. Email the Lochaber Archive Centre at lochaber.archives@highlifehighland.com and ask about collection GB3218 / L/D73, the MacColl papers. It runs about £2.75 per scanned page, emailed to you. A good deal of the collection is still unscanned, including Janet Roberton's diaries (three volumes, 1848 to 1860), two pages of Hugh's own diary from 1850, the letters that passed between the family and Rhode Island from 1929 to 1942, and a typed file of transcriptions of the Roberton letters and diaries that the archive already holds.
